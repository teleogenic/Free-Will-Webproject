The Nature Against Superintelligence

Why Computational Intelligence Is Not Possible

Nick Boström’s text on “superintelligence” assumes that intelligence is computational, functionally replicable, and independent of phenomenal subjectivity. The structural dual-aspect ontology developed in my work on biological phenomenality and teleology contradicts all three assumptions.

In this framework, phenomenality does not arise from computational or informational complexity; it arises from biological integration. Physical structure has an external aspect, fully describable by physics and biology, and an internal aspect: a proto-phenomenal potential that becomes actual only under organismic unity and metabolic self-maintenance. When a biological whole actualizes this internal aspect, a phenomenal interior appears. This interior grounds subjectivity, teleological direction, and agency.

This analysis is grounded in a biological emergence model of consciousness, which differs categorically from computationalist assumptions about intelligence. The model shows that subjectivity arises only through organismic teleology and cannot be instantiated in artificial systems.

Intelligence is not computation

Boström equates intelligence with information-processing capacity. On a structural dual-aspect view, this is a category mistake. The organism becomes a subject because the biological whole is the only configuration capable of realizing the internal aspect of structure. Intelligence is therefore not a function of operations per second, algorithmic complexity, or signal speed. It is a function of biological unity, phenomenal interiority, and internal teleology. Digital systems lack all three.

The argument demonstrates why phenomenal intentionality cannot be produced by AI functionalism, even under hypothetical recursive self-improvement. Functional outputs do not constitute a subject, and no computational architecture can generate a first-person perspective.

Phenomenal interior is necessary for cognition

Boström claims that superintelligence need not be conscious. Within a dual-aspect biology, this is ontologically impossible. Subjectivity is the phenomenal interior of life itself, not an epiphenomenon of physical processes. Agency arises when this interior expresses the organism’s intrinsic direction. Without phenomenal interiority, there is no subjectivity, no teleology, and no genuine intelligence. A system without an inner horizon cannot understand, intend, evaluate, or direct itself; it can only execute external instructions.

Biological actualization cannot be simulated

Boström assumes that whole-brain emulation is possible in principle. The structural dual-aspect model shows that phenomenality requires biological actualization, not functional replication. Phenomenality emerges only when the internal aspect is realized through biological organization: metabolic self-maintenance, systemic integration, internal causal closure, and organismic unity. Simulation is not actualization. A simulated neuron does not metabolize, self-maintain, or participate in a biological whole. No digital simulation can therefore produce a phenomenal interior.

This constitutes a non-computational consciousness argument: consciousness is not an information-processing phenomenon, and therefore the superintelligence scenario rests on a category mistake. The biological subject cannot be reduced to algorithmic structure.

Teleology cannot be programmed

In Boström’s picture, intelligence is optimization. In a biological dual-aspect ontology, teleology is intrinsic and phenomenal. Once actualized, the organism’s interior generates internal teleology: its own felt orientation toward continuation and coherence. Teleology is the organism’s own phenomenal direction, not an externally imposed objective function. Digital systems have only extrinsic goals assigned by programmers. Organisms have intrinsic goals grounded in their phenomenal interior. Teleology cannot be programmed, and computational systems cannot be genuine agents.

From the standpoint of dual-aspect biology, this explains why AI cannot be conscious: artificial systems lack organismic teleology, intrinsic normativity, and emergent phenomenal interiority. No amount of computational complexity bridges this ontological gap.

Agency and phenomenal causation

Agency is the organism’s phenomenal causal node: the way its interior modulates its external dynamics. Phenomenal causation is not a violation of physical law, but a holistic modulation of the organism’s behavior by its internal aspect. The whole directs the parts. Digital systems cannot regulate themselves phenomenally, anticipate outcomes from an internal horizon, or modify their behavior based on intrinsic meaning. They lack the structural conditions for agency.

The model therefore provides a direct critique of computationalism, showing that the assumptions behind superintelligence theory collapse once consciousness is understood as a biologically emergent, teleological phenomenon rather than a computational abstraction.

Collapse of superintelligence timelines

If intelligence requires biological actualization, phenomenal interiority, and internal teleology, then increasing computational power does not bring us closer to intelligence. Boström’s timelines are not merely optimistic or pessimistic; they are ontologically misdirected. They measure the wrong quantity. A system can grow arbitrarily powerful in computation without ever becoming a subject.

AI risk without superintelligence

Artificial systems can still be dangerous: as tools of automation, control, and systemic amplification. But they cannot become superintelligent subjects with intrinsic goals and phenomenal agency. The structural dual-aspect ontology restricts AI risk to misuse, design, and systemic effects, not to autonomous artificial minds. The existential risk scenarios built on computational superintelligence rest on an ontological confusion.

Conclusion

A structural dual-aspect ontology of mind shows that intelligence is biological, subjectivity is phenomenal, teleology is internal, and agency is organismic. Phenomenality requires biological actualization of the internal aspect of structure. Digital systems cannot become subjects. The concept of artificial superintelligence, as defined by Boström, is therefore ontologically impossible. His predictions and risk models collapse once phenomenality and teleology are treated as biological, not computational, phenomena.
